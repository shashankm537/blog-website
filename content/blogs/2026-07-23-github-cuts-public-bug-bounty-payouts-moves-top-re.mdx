# GitHub Revamps Bug Bounty Program, Shifting Rewards and Introducing an Elite Tier

GitHub is making significant changes to its public bug bounty program, aiming to streamline the process and reward top security researchers more handsomely. Starting July 27, 2026, expect a notable reduction in payouts for publicly reported vulnerabilities, but also the introduction of a more exclusive, invite-only tier for seasoned professionals.

## A Shift in Public Payouts: Lower Rewards for Broader Access

The core of the change involves a substantial decrease in the monetary rewards offered through the public bug bounty program. For critical vulnerabilities, the payout will drop from a potential $30,000+ to a fixed $10,000. Similarly, high-severity bugs will now fetch a maximum of $5,000, down from up to $20,000. This move aims to reduce the volume of less impactful submissions and encourage researchers to focus on more significant security flaws. While the exact percentages vary, reports indicate an overall reduction of at least 50% across most severity levels for the public program.

## The VIP Treatment: Exclusive Access and Higher Stakes

In tandem with the public program's adjustments, GitHub is launching a permanent "VIP Tier" for a select group of researchers. This private, invitation-only program will offer significantly higher rewards, with critical vulnerabilities potentially earning $30,000 or more. This tiered approach is designed to foster deeper relationships with top-tier security talent, providing them with faster responses and closer collaboration with GitHub's security engineering team. Eligibility for this exclusive club will be based on a track record of submitting a certain number of high-quality vulnerability reports, though specific criteria are still being finalized.

## What To Watch

The cybersecurity community will be closely observing how these changes impact the volume and quality of reported vulnerabilities. It will be interesting to see if the allure of higher payouts in the VIP tier incentivizes researchers to meet the more stringent qualification requirements, or if the reduced public payouts will deter some from participating. GitHub's stated goal is to incentivize "better" submissions over "more" submissions, and the success of this strategy will soon become apparent.
